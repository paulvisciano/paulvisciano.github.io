# Surveillance Pricing Is Just Profile-Building With a Checkout Button

The FTC just said the quiet part out loud. On August 19 it released a draft enforcement policy on personalized pricing — what regulators and advocates increasingly call surveillance pricing. Companies take browsing history, location, device signals, shopping patterns, and other personal data, then show you a different price than the person next to you. Hotels can charge more if the trip looks essential (a funeral, for example). Ride-hailing can raise the fare when the destination is a hospital. Grocery delivery can adjust the milk price because the household has children.

The agency is not banning the practice outright. It is saying that hiding it is likely deceptive under the FTC Act. Consumers reasonably expect the listed price to be the same for everyone. When it is not, and no one tells them, that is the problem the draft targets.

This is not abstract privacy talk. It is the commercial end of the same profile-building machinery that government and platforms have refined for years. Data is collected, stitched into a score of who you are and how much you will pay, then used against you at the moment of decision. The price tag becomes a personalized weapon.

I spent fifteen years shipping enterprise software at scale — Paylocity Core Mobile, then principal engineer work at OutSystems. The systems that move money and identity at that level make the incentives obvious. Data is currency. Once it leaves your device, someone else spends it.

That is why the apps I build now are websites that run on localhost first. Knowledge Graph is a private spatial memory engine: an infinite Three.js canvas where conversations, photos, and voice notes float as time layers. LightRAG and llama.cpp keep the models and the graph on the machine. Unplug the network and it still works. Where is Paul? turns a life into a navigable 3D globe and timeline — the published version is a demo; the real product is the copy you run yourself. Musical Cubes reimagines music production as rotating 3D instrument stems. All of them are PWAs. Open them in a normal browser or in Apple Vision Pro; the spatial surface is just the web.

The published demos exist so people can see the idea. The private clone is the point. Your data never has to leave the device to feed the next pricing model or the next ad profile. Crypto people already treat a seed phrase like something that must not leak. Personal data deserves the same discipline.

Surveillance pricing is simply the clearest current proof that the collection has a price tag attached to it — yours. The FTC is starting to force disclosure. The deeper move is still architectural: keep the raw material offline, on hardware you control, in tools that were designed never to phone home.
